About

I'm a second-year PhD student in Computer Science at Virginia Tech, advised by Dr. Doug Bowman and co-advised by Dr. Chris North.

Image taken from Ibrahim A. Tahmid et al., Evaluating the Feasibility of Predicting Information Relevance During Sensemaking with Eye Gaze Data (IEEE ISMAR 2023).

My long-term research goal is to bring immersive computing into everyday analytical work through interfaces that support human understanding and collaboration.

My research sits at the intersection of human-computer interaction, extended reality, and immersive analytics. I study how spatial interfaces help people organize information, understand relationships, and collaborate across time. I’m particularly interested in how people build on a partner’s reasoning in shared workspaces, and how AI can support analytical work while preserving human understanding and control.

Outside the lab, I love cooking and have been learning to bake lately. I host soccer watch parties whenever my favorite club plays, and I’m always up for a good movie, especially a Christopher Nolan film.

Understanding Asynchronous Collaborative Sensemaking Through a Multi-Session Exploratory Study in Immersive Workspaces

A. Roy, C. Connor, S. In, K. Whitley, C. North, D. Bowman

In preparation for CSCW 2027
Abstract
Abstract

Immersive workspaces allow analysts to externalize information and reasoning in persistent spatial arrangements, but how these workspaces support collaboration across time remains underexplored. We investigate asynchronous collaborative sensemaking through a multi-session exploratory study with 30 participants in 15 dyads. Partners alternated between individual sessions in a shared immersive workspace, inheriting and building on one another’s documents, notes, clusters, and links. We combined behavioral logs with semi-structured interviews to examine collaborative strategies, handoff practices, and workspace evolution. Analysts maintain continuity by selectively following, checking, and adapting inherited work. Whether this feels collaborative depends on their ability to understand their partner’s reasoning, recognize what their partner contributed, and know how to continue. Spatial groupings and authored notes offered entry points for continuation, yet incomplete explanations, unclear conventions, and accumulated visual complexity could leave incoming analysts reconstructing the reasoning behind an arrangement. These observations suggest that persistent spatial structure alone is insufficient to communicate analytical intent. We discuss implications for asynchronous immersive workspaces that make rationale, provenance, and changes legible while supporting both independent investigation and continuity between collaborators.

Selected Research Projects

Visualize Spatial Negotiation in XR

Ongoing project · IEEE VR 2027 3DUI Contest preparation
Abstract
Abstract

Remote collaborators making spatial decisions must reconcile different object placements, orientations, and environmental constraints. Existing approaches to shared manipulation and collaborator awareness provide important foundations, but unresolved spatial intentions can remain difficult to inspect and negotiate. We present an XR interaction technique that externalizes these intentions within a shared elastic structure containing editable replicas of complete object poses. Collaborators manipulate their own proposals while the structure continuously adapts to positional and rotational differences, making intermediate alternatives and disagreement visible without overwriting either proposal. Mutually feasible placement regions allow users to inspect concrete furniture replicas against their constraints before selecting a shared candidate. As proposals converge, the scaffold relaxes toward agreement; stretching, twisting, and fracture communicate increasing divergence and the need to reconfigure the negotiation. The rubber band serves as a shared negotiation scaffold connecting intentions, feasible alternatives, and emerging consensus. This ongoing project explores a unified direct-manipulation approach to translation, rotation, and constraint-aware spatial negotiation.

Research context

This project draws on prior work on collaborator-awareness cues, virtual replicas, and shared 3D object manipulation. Its focus is on making unresolved pose intentions and constraint-aware alternatives inspectable during negotiation.

  • Tian et al. (2023): Using Virtual Replicas to Improve Mixed Reality Remote Collaboration
  • Wieland et al. (2021): Separation, Composition, or Hybrid?

Subtle Gaze Guidance in VR

Cherelle Connor, Yutong Ren, Ayush Roy

Subtle Gaze Direction in Cinematic VR
Abstract
Abstract

Cinematic experiences use well-established editing techniques to guide user attention throughout narrative content. With the growing prominence of head-worn displays (HWD), there has been an increase in the use of this medium to create Cinematic Virtual Reality (CVR) experiences. However, given that CVR allows viewers to perceive content in 360 degrees, directors are no longer able to control how and when viewers see certain content within the scene. This provides an opportunity for bad actors to misguide users or misrepresent characters during the scene through the use of subtle gaze direction attacks. In this study, we investigate this threat by implementing subtle gaze direction cues within CVR scenes and examining whether they can redirect viewers’ attention away from relevant narrative content and toward attacker-selected content. We developed a subtle cue by modulating the luminance of a circular region of pixels within the scene. The cues were then placed either directly on a specific item or character within a region of interest (in-world cue) or in the periphery in order to guide the viewer to the appropriate region of interest (periphery cue). Our findings suggest that most viewers did not recognize the presence of the in-world cue and shifted their gaze toward the cue, showing a susceptibility to this type of attack. However, most of the participants did notice the periphery cue, but did not find it distracting. Overall, we believe that while the use of subtle gaze direction can provide unobtrusive guidance within CVR scenes, it also has the potential to redirect user attention and shape their perception of the content.

Research context

Related work provides the perceptual basis for subtle cues and evidence from immersive displays. This project examines their potential misuse in cinematic VR.

  • Bailey et al. (2009), Subtle Gaze Direction: Brief peripheral image modulation combined with eye tracking can redirect gaze. This provides a foundation for the luminance cue mechanism.
  • Grogorick et al. (2018), Gaze Guidance in Immersive Environments: Among five techniques tested on immersive panorama images, local luminance modulation was most effective during the first second. This motivates testing such cues in VR; it does not establish effects on narrative interpretation.
